Noise Canceling

An interactive explainer · turn your sound on, these figures make noise

Noise-canceling headphones do something that sounds impossible the first time you hear it described. They make silence by adding more sound. Not by blocking your ears, not by drowning the noise out, but by playing a second sound so precisely shaped that it erases the first one. This piece explains how, and it lets you hear every step.

A heads up: the figures below actually play audio. Find the volume you like, then start hitting the play buttons. Headphones help, but anything works.

Sound is just air, wiggling

When something vibrates, it shoves the air next to it. That air shoves the air next to it, and a ripple of pressure races outward at about 340 meters per second. When the ripple reaches your eardrum, it pushes and pulls, and your brain calls the pattern a sound.

A pure tone is the simplest version: the pressure rises and falls in a smooth, repeating curve, a sine wave. How fast it repeats is the frequency, which you hear as pitch. How tall it is, the amplitude, is how loud it feels. Play with both and listen.

Two waves add up

Here is the fact that makes everything else work. When two sound waves arrive at the same spot, the air does not pick one. It simply adds them. At every instant, the push from one wave and the push from the other combine into a single push.

If two waves rise and fall together, they reinforce and you get a louder wave. But if one wave rises exactly while the other falls, they fight. Line them up perfectly out of step, same shape, opposite direction, and they cancel completely. The pushes and pulls sum to nothing. Silence.

The orange wave below is a copy of the blue one, and you control how far out of step it is. Drag it to 180° and the black sum goes flat. Then press play and listen to it disappear.

So: listen, flip, play it back

That is the entire trick. A noise-canceling headphone is a little machine bolted to the side of your head that does three things, thousands of times a second.

A tiny microphone on the outside listens to the noise heading for your ear. A chip flips that noise upside down, turning every push into a pull. And a speaker on the inside plays the flipped version, the anti-noise, right next to your eardrum. The original noise and the anti-noise arrive together, add up, and cancel. You are left with the quiet.

Below is a droning engine hum, the kind you get on a plane. Press play to suffer through it, then switch the cancellation on. The blue anti-noise appears, the residual at your ear collapses, and the drone falls away.

Why it only kills the hum

If this worked on everything, the world would be silent on demand. It does not, and the reason is timing.

To cancel a wave you have to play its mirror image at exactly the right moment. Get the timing wrong by even a fraction of the wave's length and your anti-noise stops subtracting and starts adding. Low sounds, like an engine, have long slow waves. There is plenty of time to measure them and fire back in step, so they cancel beautifully. High sounds, like a voice or a clatter, have tiny fast waves. By the time the chip has measured one and computed its opposite, the wave has already moved on, and the anti-noise lands in the wrong place.

Slide up the frequency and watch the cancellation fall apart.

The two kinds of listening

Real headphones usually run two microphones. A feedforward mic sits on the outside and hears the noise before it arrives, giving the chip a head start. A feedback mic sits inside, next to the speaker, and listens to whatever is left after cancellation, so the system can correct its own mistakes in real time. Good headphones blend both, which is why their noise canceling feels almost alive, leaning into a steady rumble and easing off when things go quiet.

None of it is magic. It is just the same humble fact you heard a few figures ago, that two equal and opposite waves add up to nothing, executed very fast and very precisely a few millimeters from your ear.
